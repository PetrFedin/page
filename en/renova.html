01 — The problem

Renovations run on chat threads, and no one is accountable

The economics of a renovation are never in one place: the estimate in a file, the conversation in a messenger, the receipts in a pile.

  1. The estimate drifts from actual costs

    The estimate lives in a file; purchasing and payments sit elsewhere. Every small difference piles up unseen until it becomes an overspend.

  2. Payment is not tied to results

    Payment follows a verbal agreement rather than a signed-off stage. What exactly was paid for cannot be checked later.

  3. The contractor’s legal status is unconfirmed

    Self-employed or sole-trader status is taken on trust and, as a rule, not checked at the time of payment.

  4. Whether a stage is finished is a matter of trust, not fact

    The crew and the client mean different things by “done”, and there is no shared confirmation. Photos, if any, stay in a phone gallery, not linked to a stage or date.

A typical case

The crew asked for an advance on materials, and the client sent it to a phone number. The materials cost less than estimated and the difference was never discussed: there was no reporting.

By handover the budget was over the estimate, and it was impossible to tell which stage caused the overspend.

Outcome: the client learns the real cost of the renovation only after the money has been spent.

02 — The principle

Every action recalculates the figures

  1. The estimate is a state, not a document

    Every change to the estimate is saved as a new version. You can see what changed, when, and why the plan departed from actual costs.

  2. Payment is tied to sign-off

    A stage payment is confirmed only after sign-off, with photo evidence and confirmation from both sides.

  3. Checks go to official sources

    Receipts and self-employed status are matched against Federal Tax Service data, not accepted on the contractor’s word. The result decides whether this contractor can be paid. The code and tests are ready; I will switch on the live check once I have the Federal Tax Service keys.

03 — System class

A place between the group chat and the contractor’s accounting system

Private renovations are run in one of three ways: a spreadsheet with receipts, a group chat with no single version of the estimate, or the contractor’s own accounting system (1C, a site manager’s software), closed to the client. None of them shows the client the full economics of the project.

The Renova column shows the intended scope; readiness is described in section 07. None of the three usual methods checks the receipt or the contractor’s status.

04 — Scope

From estimate to signed-off result

The system holds the order of steps: until a stage is signed off, its payment cannot be confirmed, and every change to the estimate leaves a trace.

  1. Estimate
  2. Version approval
  3. Work on the stage
  4. Photo evidence
  5. Sign-off
  6. Payment
  7. Next stage

05 — Roles

Client and contractor each get their own screens

The client and the contractor see the same site, but each sees their own part.

  1. Client

    Sees the whole estimate, its versions and deviations, stage status and payment history. Signs off a stage or rejects it with a comment.

  2. Contractor or crew

    Runs their own area: photographs the stage, requests sign-off and confirms receipt of payment.

  3. Renovation company

    Runs several sites at once and sees the estimate, stages and payments for each.

A typical case

The client went away for two weeks while the finishing stage was under way. The crew sent photos and a sign-off request from the app. The client checked the stage on their phone and signed it off with one snagging comment, without visiting the site.

Payment was confirmed the same day: no phone calls and no downtime because of the trip.

09 — FAQ

Frequently asked questions

How does this differ from a spreadsheet and a messenger chat?
The estimate, stage and payment are connected: the crew requests sign-off from the app, the client signs off the stage from the photos, and only then does payment go through. In a spreadsheet or a chat, that chain rests on a verbal agreement.
How is the contractor’s legal status checked?
By the system, not by the client: the contractor sends nothing, and the check runs before payment. The code and tests are ready; live verification awaits keys.
Do I have to move the whole renovation into the app at once?
No. Start with one stage or one estimate and run the rest as you usually do.
What happens if the connection is lost on site?
Actions go into an offline queue and are sent once the connection returns; stage sign-off and stage photos, among others, work this way. Making sure a resent action is never applied twice after a lost server response is still being finished.
Who pays for the app, the client or the contractor?
I will set the payment model after the pilots: the side that gains more on site should pay.
Can the client change the estimate alone, without the contractor’s agreement?
Once a contractor is appointed, the estimate is locked on the contractor’s proposal with the client’s confirmation. After that, changes are raised as variation orders that both sides agree; every version is visible to both.
Can I see a demo?
Yes, on the working build.
